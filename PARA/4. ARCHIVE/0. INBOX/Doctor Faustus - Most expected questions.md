## **On Character and Ambition**

##### 1. **Faustus's pride propels him towards greatness and yet, at the same time, it also leads him to his damnation. Do you agree?**
I strongly agree with the statement. In Christopher Marlowe’s _Doctor Faustus_, **pride functions as a "double-edged weapon" designed to both elevate the protagonist to heroic dimensions and ultimately ensure his spiritual damnation**. The play brilliantly captures the tension between the Renaissance celebration of human potential and the medieval Christian morality that condemns human presumption.

From the very beginning, **Faustus’s pride propels him towards greatness through his "aspiring mind" and his immense intellectual achievements**. He has mastered philosophy, medicine, law, and divinity, yet he finds all these traditional human disciplines fundamentally inadequate for his restless spirit. His pride makes him deeply dissatisfied with his mortal boundaries; as he famously laments, "yet art thou still Faustus and a man". Driven by a heroic desire to transcend these human limitations, he seeks "a world of profit and delight, of power, of honour, of omnipotence". By turning to black magic, Faustus embodies the bold, adventurous spirit of the Renaissance, willing to risk his very soul to become a "demi-god". The Chorus compares him to the mythical Icarus, highlighting the absolute splendour and poignancy of this magnificent human flight toward curious knowledge and infinite greatness.

However, **the tragedy lies in the fact that this exact same pride is the instrument of his catastrophic fall**. From a Christian and moral angle, Faustus perverts his own human intelligence by deliberately asking for art to serve his self-conceit rather than the divine will. His pride blinds him to his spiritual reality, making him terrifyingly complacent. When Mephostophilis explicitly warns him that Lucifer and his fellow spirits fell because of "aspiring pride and insolence," Faustus refuses to look in the mirror and see that he is foolishly re-enacting Lucifer's own damnation. Instead of heeding the warning, his arrogance leads him to patronisingly tell the demon to bear his losses with "manly fortitude," completely dismissing the horrifying reality of hell as a mere "fable".

Furthermore, **Faustus’s pride eventually debases him, proving that his pursuit of greatness was a tragic illusion**. As the play progresses, it becomes powerfully clear that "power corrupts and debases, especially, a proud and presumptuous man like Faustus". Instead of becoming a great emperor or achieving his noble national aspirations, his pride degrades him into a mere court entertainer and a petty trickster. He wastes his omnipotence on farcical exploits—such as playing invisible pranks on the Pope, clapping horns on the head of a mocking knight, and swindling a poor horse-courser. These actions serve as a bitter commentary on his foolish bargain; he is doomed to lose his eternal soul without ever truly gaining the magnificent world he set out to conquer.

In his final hours, **his pride transforms into a paralysing despair that cements his damnation**. Although the allegorical Old Man and the Good Angel offer him late chances for salvation, Faustus has gone so deeply into evil by his own willful choice that he lacks the humility and faith to seek divine grace. As the Good Angel explicitly points out before his final doom, it is his arrogant love of the world that sealed his fate: "Hadst thou affected sweet divinity / Hell or the devil had had no power on thee". Ultimately, the tragedy of Doctor Faustus is that **the very pride that sparks his heroic imagination and propels him toward infinite greatness is the exact flaw that guarantees his inescapable destruction**.

##### 2. **Do you think what Faustus does with his powers match the ambitions displayed in Act I? Does it make Faustus a lesser man?**

**The trajectory of Christopher Marlowe’s protagonist in _Doctor Faustus_ is marked by a stark and tragic contrast between his initial grand aspirations and the trivial reality of his magical exploits.** It is undeniably true that what Faustus actually achieves with his powers fails miserably to match the heroic ambitions he displays in Act I. This tragic discrepancy degrades him, effectively making him a lesser man than he was before he signed the demonic pact.

**The Grandeur of Act I Ambitions** In his opening soliloquy, Faustus is dissatisfied with the boundaries of traditional human knowledge—logic, medicine, law, and divinity. He turns to necromancy because he dreams of acquiring "a world of profit and delight, / of power, of honour, of omnipotence". At this stage, his imagination is heroic and boundless; he envisions himself as a "demi-god" with absolute command over nature and spirits. Furthermore, his ambitions are not merely personal but nationalistic and grand in scope: he plans to wall all of Germany with brass, dress university students in fine Indian silk, chase the invading Prince of Parma out of the land, and ultimately become the "great emperor of the world".

**The Reality of His Exploits** However, once he signs the contract with Lucifer, the middle scenes of the play (Acts III and IV) reveal a disastrous shortfall. **Instead of conquering kingdoms, Faustus wastes his supernatural powers on farcical and petty exploits**. His actions become increasingly trivial:

- In Rome, rather than confronting the religious establishment with profound power, he becomes invisible and plays childish tricks on the Pope, snatching his food and throwing fireworks at friars.
- In the court of Emperor Charles IV, he acts as a mere court entertainer, conjuring up the spirits of Alexander the Great and his paramour—illusions that Faustus himself admits "are but shadows, not substantial".
- He uses his magic to take petty, sadistic revenge by clapping horns on the head of Benvolio, a knight who mocked him.
- He stoops to the level of a common con artist, swindling a poor horse-courser out of forty dollars by selling him a horse that turns into a bundle of hay in the water.
- His final display of power before his doom is simply fetching out-of-season grapes for the Duchess of Vanholt.

**Degeneration into a "Lesser Man"** These actions confirm that Faustus’s bargain was a foolish one and that his newly acquired power drastically diminishes his stature. **As the study material aptly notes, "Faustus, who aspired to be a king has become a clown"**. His clowning, swindling, and practical jokes serve as a clear index of his moral and intellectual degeneration. The play illustrates that power corrupts and debases, especially a proud and presumptuous man like Faustus.

Initially, Faustus complained that despite his vast academic achievements, he was "still but Faustus, and a man". He sought magic to transcend his humanity. Yet, **having gained powers of magic, he becomes arguably _less_ than the respected, brilliant scholar he once was**. He never achieves the political power he craved, nor does he gain infinite knowledge, as Mephostophilis even refuses to answer his questions about who made the world.

**Conclusion** In conclusion, Faustus's actions with his powers completely contradict his initial ambitions. **He trades his eternal soul to become a master of the universe, but settles for being a petty trickster who deals in cheap illusions.** The tragic irony of _Doctor Faustus_ is that the very power he sought to elevate himself instead debases him, making him a lesser man who loses the world he aimed to conquer and his own soul in the process.


##### 3. **What do Doctor Faustus and Lucifer have in common?**

In Christopher Marlowe's _Doctor Faustus_, **the most significant trait that Doctor Faustus and Lucifer have in common is their fatal flaw of "aspiring pride and insolence"**. This shared characteristic forms the theological and moral backbone of the play, illustrating how the protagonist tragically re-enacts the devil's own original sin.

When Faustus successfully conjures Mephostophilis in Act I, he explicitly questions the demon about Lucifer and the reasons for his fall from grace. Mephostophilis plainly reveals that **Lucifer and his companion spirits fell because of their "aspiring pride and insolence"**. Through this candid explanation, **Mephostophilis is essentially holding up a mirror to Faustus, giving him a terrifyingly honest warning about the exact sin he is currently committing**. However, Faustus is too complacent and blinded by his own ambition to look in this mirror; he fails to see that he is foolishly re-enacting Lucifer's catastrophic fall.

Furthermore, both characters share a profound dissatisfaction with their assigned boundaries, leading them to actively rebel against the divine order. Just as Lucifer and his unhappy spirits "conspired against our God" to challenge the supreme authority of Heaven, Faustus perverts his human intelligence and turns away from the path of salvation to challenge the limits set by God on human knowledge and power. Critics like Maxwell argue that **the central sin of Faustus is precisely this pride—the exact same sin that led to the fall of Lucifer**. Both figures act as arrogant overreachers who rebel against the immutable laws of creation, effectively becoming usurpers against God.

Consequently, both Lucifer and Faustus share the exact same tragic punishment: eternal damnation and permanent separation from divine grace. Mephostophilis explains to Faustus that hell is not merely a physical location, but a spiritual state of being permanently "deprived of its eternal joys and everlasting bliss". Because both Lucifer and Faustus willfully reject God, they are doomed to suffer this perpetual psychological and spiritual torment. By the end of the play, **Faustus's final hours serve as a direct re-enactment of Lucifer's fall, driven by the very same "roaring pride"**.

In conclusion, **what Faustus and Lucifer fundamentally share is a tragic trajectory defined by arrogant rebellion.** Faustus's desperate desire to become a "demi-god" through dark magic perfectly mirrors Lucifer's original cosmic insolence. The play demonstrates that Faustus does not achieve unique greatness, but rather blindly repeats the oldest sin in Christian theology, sealing his own inescapable doom.

## **On Dramatic Elements and Structure**

##### 4. Faustus's soliloquy is followed by the appearance of the Good Angel and the Bad Angel. **Do you think this pattern has any significance for the plot or character?**

Yes, the recurrent pattern of the Good Angel and the Bad Angel appearing during or after Faustus’s soliloquies holds profound significance for both the psychological development of the character and the overall structure of the plot.

**Externalisation of Inner Conflict** For Faustus’s character, the two angels act as an **externalisation of the two conflicting voices within his own conscience**. Whenever Faustus is left alone with his thoughts and experiences moments of doubt or despair, the angels materialise to signal this intense inner moral struggle. At a psychological level, their simultaneous appearances represent the active dilemma of his conscience: the terrifying choice between seeking divine grace or adopting a definitively evil course.

**Reinforcing the Theme of Deliberate Choice** From a thematic and plot perspective, the angels continually reinforce the significance of Faustus's deliberate choices. The Good Angel consistently reiterates the orthodox moral view, urging Faustus to lay aside his "damned book" and avoid God's wrath. In contrast, the Bad Angel echoes the heroic and hubristic ambitions of Faustus's own soliloquies, repeatedly encouraging him to "go forward in that famous art" and pursue absolute power. This interplay serves as a physical staging of the **Morality play tradition, showcasing the battle between good and evil over the protagonist's soul**.

**Tracking Faustus's Spiritual Degeneration** The pattern of their appearances serves as a structural tool to map Faustus’s tragic decline. As the plot progresses, the conflict between these two voices grows feebler, with the influence of the Bad Angel growing noticeably stronger.

- In Act I, Scene v, when Faustus is assailed by despair while waiting to sign the demonic pact, the Bad Angel speaks first, and Faustus's doubts quickly evaporate at the mere mention of wealth and power.
- Later in Scene vi, their appearance further confounds his mind, leading to his helpless realisation: "My heart is harden'd, I cannot repent". In this same scene, when the Good Angel momentarily succeeds in making Faustus cry out to Christ, it directly drives the plot forward by prompting the immediate arrival of Lucifer, Beelzebub, and Mephostophilis, who step in to terrorise Faustus and secure their hold over him.

**The Final, Fatal Variation of the Pattern** The ultimate significance of this pattern is revealed in how it shifts at the very end of the play. In his final hours, the two Angels visit Faustus for the last time, but significantly, **they enter "not together but at different doors"**. Because their previous simultaneous appearances represented the active, ongoing dilemma of his conscience, **their separate entry structurally signifies that the inner dilemma is no longer there**. The time for making a choice has completely passed. The Good Angel, having grown feebler, merely speaks of the celestial happiness Faustus has permanently lost, while the Bad Angel invites him to stare into the vast, perpetual torture-house of Hell.

Ultimately, this dramatic pattern constantly reminds the audience that **Faustus is not a passive victim of fate, but a man who repeatedly and consciously ignores his own moral conscience** to walk wilfully toward his own damnation.

##### 5. **Discuss the role of the chorus in the play.**

In Christopher Marlowe's _Doctor Faustus_, the Chorus serves several vital dramatic and thematic functions, guiding the audience's understanding of the protagonist and managing the play's narrative flow. Its roles can be broken down into the following key areas:

- **Defining the Scope and Direction:** The play opens with a Prologue delivered by the Chorus, which immediately establishes the unique nature of the tragedy. **The Chorus clarifies that the play will not focus on traditional themes like "heroic wars or romantic love in 'high places'," but rather on the life and career of an ordinary German scholar named Faustus**. By using the metaphor of the mythical Icarus—who flew too close to the sun and melted his waxen wings—the Chorus highlights Faustus's dangerous ambition and reveals Marlowe's ambivalent attitude, showing both awe and contempt for the fallen hero.
- **Summing Up and Pronouncing Judgement:** Functioning as a moral compass, the Chorus summarizes Faustus's life and explicitly comments on his actions. **It tells the audience that Faustus's downfall was the direct result of his "pride and presumption"**. By stating that Faustus willingly adopted an evil course and deserved his tragic punishment, the Chorus frames the play within a Christian moral context.
- **Bridging Time and Space:** From a practical staging perspective, the Chorus acts as a narrative bridge to overcome the physical limitations of the Elizabethan stage. **It appears between acts to describe events that span vast distances and periods, such as Faustus’s journey across cosmic space**. Later, the second Chorus steps in to update the audience on Faustus's spreading fame, his respected position at Emperor Charles IV’s court in Germany, and the admiration he earns from friends for his astronomical knowledge.
- **Concluding the Moral Lesson:** At the end of the play, the Chorus delivers the Epilogue, bringing the tragedy to a fitting close by blending its Heroic and Morality play elements. **The Chorus provides a final, solemn warning against the dangers of following "forward wits" who "practise more than heavenly power permits,"** reinforcing the play's core message while acknowledging the tragic wonder of delving into unlawful things.
