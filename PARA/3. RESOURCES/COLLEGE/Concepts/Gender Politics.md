# Gender Politics

Eighteenth-century English society prescribed sharply different roles for men and women, governing female conduct through the interlocking codes of honour, modesty, and marriageability. Literature of the period both reproduces and interrogates these codes.

## Women's Social Position

- **The honour system** — a woman's social worth was defined by sexual reputation; honour, once lost, was permanent and catastrophic
- **The marriage market** — women had few economic options outside marriage; they were exchanged between families as property
- **Domesticity** — the ideal woman was modest, obedient, domestic; public speech or ambition were suspect
- **Appearance as power** — beauty was the currency women could trade, but it gave only indirect, temporary influence (Belinda's dilemma)

Feminist critics (e.g., Ellen Pollak) have examined how 18th-century texts construct women as objects of male desire and social management — even while seemingly celebrating female wit and beauty.

## In the Texts

- **[[The Rape of the Lock]]** — Belinda possesses brilliance, wit, and beauty, yet her "honour" is literalised as a lock of hair Pope both satirises the system and participates in it; the sylphs allegorise the social surveillance that governs female conduct → [[Allegory]]
  > "If to her share some female errors fall, / Look on her face, and you'll forget 'em all." (II.17–18)
- **[[The Vicar of Wakefield]]** — Olivia Primrose is seduced and bigamously "married" by Squire Thornhill; her ruin is treated as a catastrophe but also sentimentalised; female virtue is fragile and victimisable → [[Family Morality]]
- **[[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]]** — women appear almost exclusively as objects of disgust or irrelevance; the Brobdingnagian ladies' bodies, seen in magnified close-up, are grotesque; female characters are absent from the rational utopia of the Houyhnhnms

## Critical Debates

- **Pope and feminism** — Ellen Pollak argues Pope's celebration of Belinda ultimately reinscribes male control; others see Pope as genuinely sympathetic to female constraints
- **Goldsmith's ambivalence** — does Goldsmith critique the honour system or simply exploit Olivia's suffering as sentimental pathos?
