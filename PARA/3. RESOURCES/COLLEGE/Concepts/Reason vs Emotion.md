# Reason vs Emotion

The tension between reason and emotion — between rational judgement and feeling, sentiment, and passion — is one of the defining dialectics of eighteenth-century English literature and thought.

> [!TIP] Reason vs. Emotion Across Tragedies
> The conflict between rational calculation and feeling drives multiple tragedies:
> - **[[Macbeth - William Shakespeare|Macbeth]]**: Ambition (reason-driven goal) vs. conscience (emotional resistance) — Macbeth chooses rationally, suffers emotionally
> - **[[Doctor Faustus - Christopher Marlowe|Doctor Faustus]]**: Reason pushing toward forbidden knowledge vs. terror of damnation
> - **18th Century**: Houyhnhnms represent pure reason without compassion; The Vicar of Wakefield privileges feeling as moral authority

## The Enlightenment Position

The dominant Enlightenment ideal held that reason should govern emotion: passions are unreliable, interest corrupts, feeling misleads. The proper response to moral questions is cool, impartial judgement — not sympathy or sentiment.

## The Sentimental Counter-Movement

Simultaneously, the sentimental tradition — developed in moral philosophy (Hutcheson, Shaftesbury) and in the novel — argued that *feeling* is the primary basis of moral action. Sympathy, benevolence, and emotional responsiveness train the moral agent better than abstract reason.

## The Three Authors' Positions

| Author               | Stance                                                                                                     |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [[Alexander Pope]]   | Champions rational moderation and *wit* — but emotion drives his satire's energy                           |
| [[Jonathan Swift]]   | Attacks the abuse of reason (Laputans, Projectors) while deeply suspicious of sentimental ideology         |
| [[Oliver Goldsmith]] | Embraces sentimental feeling as moral authority, but maintains ironic distance from Dr Primrose's excesses |
## In the Texts

- **[[The Rape of the Lock]]** — Belinda's "passion" (fury over the lock) is excessive; Clarissa's speech counsels rational acceptance; the poem's comedy depends on disproportionate emotional response → [[Satire]]
- **[[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]]** — the Houyhnhnms embody perfect reason *without* passion or sympathy; their cold perfection may itself be a satirical target; the Yahoos embody pure appetite → [[Human Nature in Swift]]
- **[[The Vicar of Wakefield]]** — Dr Primrose's patient feeling is the moral centre; the novel appeals explicitly to the reader's sympathetic imagination → [[Sentimental Novel]]
