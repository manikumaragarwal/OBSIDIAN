# Macbeth — Hamartia (Tragic Flaw)

> [!INFO] Aristotelian Tragedy
> According to [[Aristotle's Poetics|Aristotle]], every tragic hero has a **hamartia** — a fatal flaw or error that leads to their downfall. Macbeth is the classic example.

---

## What is Hamartia?

The **Greek term** "hamartia" literally means "missing the mark" — an archery term used metaphorically for:
- A **fatal flaw** in character (like pride, ambition)
- An **error in judgment** (a mistake)
- A **weakness** that destiny exploits

In Aristotle's theory, the hero must:
1. Be of **noble status** (high to begin with)
2. Have a **hamartia** that causes their fall
3. Experience **[[Peripeteia|reversal of fortune]]**
4. Have an **[[Anagnorisis|moment of recognition]]**
5. Create **[[Catharsis|emotional release]]** in the audience

---

## Macbeth's Hamartia — Dual Weakness

Macbeth's tragic flaw is **not simple** — it's a combination of weaknesses:

| Flaw | Evidence | Effect |
|------|----------|--------|
| **Ambition** | "I have no spur... but only vaulting ambition" (1.7) | Drives him toward murder |
| **Suggestibility** | Easily influenced by Lady M & witches | Cannot say no to temptation |
| **Imagination** | Sees daggers, ghosts | Makes guilt unbearable |

### Ambition — The Core Problem

> "I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself / And falls on the other."

**Analysis**:
- Shakespeare shows Macbeth is **self-aware** — he KNOWS ambition is his problem
- The **horse imagery** ("o'erleaps itself, and falls") shows ambition is reckless
- The verb **"o'erleap"** suggests jumping over moral boundaries
- "**Falls on the other**" means it leads to disaster

**Crucial distinction**: Macbeth is NOT a VICTIM of ambition — he CHOOSES to follow it. This makes the tragedy **more pitiable**.

### Suggestibility — The Second Flaw

Macbeth is **uniquely susceptible** to influence:

| Influencer | Method | Effect on Macbeth |
|------------|--------|-------------------|
| **Witches** | Prophecy | Triggers existing ambition |
| **Lady Macbeth** | Manipulation ("When you durst do it, then you were a man") | Forces action he doubts |
| **Society** | Pressure to be "a man" | Equates masculinity with violence |

**Why this matters**: Macbeth is NOT naturally evil — he's **weak-willed**. His suggestibility makes him a **tragic figure** rather than a villain.

> [!NOTE] Contrast with Other Tragic Heroes
> - **Oedipus**: **Driven by fate** to unknowingly commit his crimes
> - **Macbeth**: **Chooses** to commit crimes though he knows they're wrong
> - **Faustus** ([[Doctor Faustus - Christopher Marlowe]]): **Signs a contract** — explicit choice
> - **Macbeth's uniqueness**: **Aware** of the choice but **unwilling** to stop

## How Hamartia Triggers the Tragedy

### The Chain of Causality

```
Hamartia (Ambition + Suggestibility)
    ↓ (creates)
Temptation (Witches' prophecy)
    ↓ (activates)
Moral Struggle (Soliloquy 1.7)
    ↓ (leads to)
Decision to Kill Duncan
    ↓ (causes)
Murder → Guilt → Paranoia
    ↓ (results in)
More Murder → Tyranny → Death
```

Every major catastrophe in the play stems from Macbeth's **initial weakness**.

## Is Macbeth Responsible? — The Central Debate

| Argument | Supporting Evidence | Rebuttal |
|----------|--------------------|----------|
| **YES — He acted freely** | He could have said no | He was manipulated — his choice was constrained |
| **NO — He was controlled** | Witches prophesied his rise | Prophecy is not compulsion — Macbeth CHOSE murder |
| **BOTH — Complex agency** | Shakespeare keeps it ambiguous | This is the best answer |

> [!TIP] Essay Strategy | When asked about responsibility:
> 1. Acknowledge the **force of external influences** (witches, Lady Macbeth)
> 2. Show Macbeth's **conscious choice** in the soliloquy in 1.7
> 3. Conclude that Shakespeare presents **complex agency** — neither pure victim nor pure villain

## Hamartia in Other Tragedies — Comparison

| Tragedy | Hero | Hamartia | Key Difference |
|---------|------|----------|----------------|
| **Oedipus the King** | Oedipus | **Blindness** to truth | Cannot escape fate |
| **Doctor Faustus** | Faustus | **Pride/Ambition** for knowledge | Explicit pact, explicit choice |
| **Antigone** ([[Antigone - Sophocles]]) | Antigone | Pride/Obedience to divine law | Chooses morality over power |
| **Macbeth** | Macbeth | Ambition + Suggestibility | Awareness + unwillingness to change |

## Catharsis — Audience's Emotional Response

Aristotle argued that tragedy creates **pity** and **terror** in the audience, leading to **emotional purification** ([[Catharsis]]).

### Pity — Why We Feel for Macbeth

| Reason | Evidence |
|--------|----------|
| **He struggles** — long soliloquy showing conscience | "If it were done when 'tis done" |
| **He suffers** — guilt, hallucinations, paranoia | "Is this a dagger?" |
| **He's aware** — knows his own flaw | "Vaulting ambition..." |

We feel pity because Macbeth is **not naturally evil** — he's a **good man** whom weakness destroys.

### Terror — Why We Fear

| What Makes Us Afraid | Effect |
|----------------------|--------|
| **Any of us could be Macbeth** | We all have flaws that could lead to disaster |
| **Fate is inescapable** | We cannot control all circumstances |
| **Guilt is permanent** | Even "all Neptune's ocean" cannot murder sleep |

> [!NOTE] Catharsis in Practice
> The audience cries for Macbeth because he is **a mirror** — we see our own potential for weakness. As he descends into madness, we release our own fears through his story.

## How Hamartia Relates to Macbeth's End

### Final Recognition ([[Anagnorisis]])

The "Tomorrow" soliloquy is Macbeth's **moment of recognition**:

> "Life's but a walking shadow... a tale / Told by an idiot, signifying nothing."

This shows:
1. He recognizes ambition was **meaningless**
2. He accepts his fate with **dignity**
3. He chooses **resistance** in the final battle ("Lay on, Macduff")

**Aristotle's requirement for tragedy**: The hero must have **knowledge of their error** before the end. Macbeth DOES have this knowledge in the final act.
