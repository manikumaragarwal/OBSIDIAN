# Macbeth — Character Analysis

> [!INFO] Character Web
> The relationships in Macbeth form a complex web of betrayal, guilt, and ambition. Each character represents different facets of the play's central themes.

---

## Macbeth — The Tragic Hero

> [!SUMMARY] The Transformation
> From "Bellona's bridegroom" to "this dead butcher" — a study in how unchecked ambition destroys the soul.

### Character Arc — By Stage

| Stage of Play | Macbeth's State | Key Evidence |
|---------------|-----------------|--------------|
| **Beginning** | Noble, respected warrior | "Brave Macbeth—well he deserves that name" |
| **After Prophecy** | Tempted but conflicted | "I am Thane of Cawdor — If good, why do I yield to that suggestion?" |
| **After Murder** | Guilt-ridden, paranoid | "Macbeth does murder sleep"; "Will all great Neptune's ocean..." |
| **Post-Banquet** | Ruthless tyrant | "From this moment / The very firstlings of my heart shall be / The firstlings" |
| **Final** | Despairing but dignified | "I 'gin to be aweary of the sun"; "Lay on, Macduff" |

### His [[Hamartia|Tragic Flaw]]

Macbeth's fatal flaw is a **dual weakness**:

1. **Ambition** — The desire for power that eclipses all else
2. **Suggestibility** — The inability to resist Lady Macbeth and the witches

> "I have no spur / To prick the sides of my intent, but only / Vaulting ambition."

This means Macbeth is **aware** of his flaw but cannot control it — making his tragedy more pitiable than if he were simply evil.

### Is He Truly Responsible?

| Perspective | Argument |
|-------------|----------|
| **YES — He chose** | He could have said no to the witches, Lady Macbeth, and his ambition |
| **NO — He was manipulated** | The witches' prophecies and Lady Macbeth's pressure made his choice inevitable |
| **BOTH — Complex agency** | Shakespeare presents him as BOTH active agent AND victim of circumstances |

> [!NOTE] Compare with Faustus
> Both [[Doctor Faustus - Christopher Marlowe|Faustus]] and Macbeth make **pacts with supernatural forces**. But:
> - Faustus signs a **written contract** (more explicit choice)
> - Macbeth has **no formal agreement**, only a tempting prophecy
> - This makes Macbeth's responsibility MORE ambiguous — did he really "choose"?

## Lady Macbeth — The Collapse

> [!SUMMARY] From Unsexed Spirit to Broken Woman
> Lady Macbeth's journey is the most psychologically complex in the play. She forces her own destruction.

### Character Transformation

| Phase | Evidence | Interpretation |
|-------|----------|----------------|
| **Reading letter** | "Too full o' the milk of human kindness" | She SEES Macbeth's flaw and aims to exploit it |
| **Invoking spirits** | "Unsex me here... take my milk for gall" | She believes rejecting femininity = strength |
| **Persuading Macbeth** | "I have given suck... I would dash the brains out" | Uses extreme imagery that likely isn't literal |
| **After murder** | "A little water clears us of this deed" | Complete denial of psychological reality |
| **Banquet scene** | "Sit worthy friends" | Takes charge, appears in control |
| **Sleep-walking** | "Out, damned spot!" | Guilt finally breaks through |
| **Offstage death** | "She should have died hereafter" | Denied even the dignity of visible death |

### Why She Breaks — Feminist Reading

Lady Macbeth represents the **cost of female transgression** in a patriarchal society:

> [!TIP] The Cost of Ambition for Women
> - Men who pursue power are **heroes** or **villains** (active agents)
> - Women who pursue power must become **inhuman** ("unsex me")
> - When the violence becomes real, they **break** because it violates their feminine nature

This shows Shakespeare's critique: society ALLOWS ambition in men but PUNISHES it in women.

### The Imaginary Child

> "I have given suck, and know / How tender 'tis to love the babe that milks me...

This is the **most mysterious line** in the play:

**Analysis**:
- Did she actually kill her child? Most critics say NO — this is **rhetorical violence**, not literal
- But we NEVER know — Shakespeare keeps it ambiguous
- The imagery shows she understands what LOSS means, making her later guilt more understandable

> [!NOTE] Compare with Other Female Characters
> - **Antigone** ([[Antigone - Sophocles]]): Chooses MORAL duty over law, accepts death
> - **Lady Macbeth**: Chooses POWER over morality, cannot accept the consequences
> - **Draupadi** ([[Mahabharata]]): Uses SPEECH to fight, not VIOLENCE

## The Weird Sisters — Fate or Catalyst?

> "When shall we three meet again? / In thunder, lightning, or in rain?"

The witches exist at the **intersection** of:
- **External fate**: Prophecies that seem to control destiny
- **Internal desire**: Macbeth's own ambition that responds to them

### Their Prophecies — Chart

| Scene | To Whom | What They Say | Result |
|-------|---------|---------------|--------|
| 1.3 | Macbeth | Thane of Glamis (true), Thane of Cawdor (becomes true), King (ambition triggered) | Sets the tragedy in motion |
| 1.3 | Banquo | "Thou shalt get kings, though thou be none" | Seeds jealousy between friends |
| 4.1 | Macbeth | Beware Macduff, None of woman born, Birnam Wood moves | False security — traps Macbeth |

### Purpose — Critical Debate

| Interpretation | Supporting Evidence | Counter-argument |
|----------------|--------------------|-----------------|
| **They control fate** | They speak truth; Macbeth's actions follow their words | Macbeth ACTS on their words — free will remains |
| **They merely reveal** | They say "nothing is, but what is not" — they show possibilities | They actively tempt with "when the hurlyburly's done" |
| **They're psychological** | Only those who seek them see them | They have supernatural powers beyond imagination |

> [!IMPORTANT] Shakespeare's Verdict (Probably)
> Shakespeare keeps them **ambiguous** because the play is about the **Renaissance debate**:
> - Do we have **free will**?
> - Or are we controlled by **fate/stars/providence**?

Neither answer is fully correct — we are caught BETWEEN.

## Macduff — The Moral Avenger

> "Lay on, Macduff, / And damn'd be him that first cried, 'Hold, enough!'"

Macduff is the **moral center** of the play, representing:
- Righteous vengeance (not petty revenge)
- Connection to natural family (his wife and children)
- The exceptional individual who breaks ordinary rules ("none of woman born")

### What Makes Him Special

| Trait | Why It Matters |
|-------|----------------|
| "None of woman born" | Represents the EXCEPTION that proves the RULE — fate can be disrupted |
| Lost his family | His revenge is **personal**, not political |
| "Not for their own demerits, but for mine" | Takes on the burden of JUSTICE, not vengeance |
| First to suspect Macbeth | Shows moral intuition over the court's blindness |

### Macduff vs. Macbeth — The Mirror Image

| Aspect | Macduff | Macbeth |
|--------|---------|---------|
| Family status | Has wife & children (LOSES them) | "Barren sceptre" (children never grow) |
| Motivation | Justice for wrong done | Ambition for power |
| Violence | Targeted, purposeful | Escalating, indiscriminate |
| Ending | Helped restore order | Brings order through death |

This is **chiasmus** (mirror structure) — Macbeth causes what Macduff suffers, and Macduff ends what Macbeth began.

## Banquo — The Ghost of Truth

> "Thou shalt get kings, though thou be none."

Banquo is the **loyal friend** who becomes Macbeth's victim, representing:
- Honest suspicion (he suspects the witches)
- The unfulfilled potential (his descendants WILL be kings)
- The haunting presence of conscience

### Why Banquo Must Die

| Reason | Evidence |
|--------|----------|
| **He suspects** | "And oftentimes, to win us to our harm, / The instruments of darkness tell us truths" |
| **His line will succeed** | The prophecy threatens Macbeth's legacy |
| **His ghost haunts** | Macbeth's guilt manifests as Banquo's specter at the banquet |

---

## Duncan — The Saintly King

> "This Duncan / Hath borne his faculties so meek..."

Duncan represents:
- The ideal monarch (gentle, trusting, virtuous)
- The natural ORDER that Macbeth violates
- The innocence that makes the murder especially tragic

### Why His Death Feels Wrong

| Element | Significance |
|---------|---------------|
| He calls Macbeth "O worthiest cousin" | Shows trust makes betrayal possible |
| He stays at Inverness (Macbeth's castle) | Violation of SACRED hospitality |
| He's "meek" and "clear" | Killing a GOOD man is worse than killing a bad one |

This makes Duncan's murder a **sacrilege** — not just a crime.
