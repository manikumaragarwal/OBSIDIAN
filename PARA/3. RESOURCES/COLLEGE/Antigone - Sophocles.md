> [!NOTE] Aristotelian Tragedy in Antigone
> Antigone is a perfect [[Aristotle's Poetics|Aristotelian tragedy]]. Apply the [[3 Tragic Beats]]:
> - **Peripeteia**: Creon's punishment of Antigone triggers the chain of deaths — his authority reversed into catastrophe
> - **Anagnorisis**: Creon realises (too late) that divine law supersedes civic law — his blindness is revealed
> - **Catharsis**: The audience feels the weight of Antigone's sacrifice and Creon's punishment — justice served but too late

> [!TIP] Cross-Text Comparisons
> - **Antigone vs. Lysistrata** ([[Lysistrata - Aristophanes|Lysistrata]]): Both feature women challenging male authority. Antigone chooses personal moral law; Lysistrata uses collective political action.
> - **Antigone vs. Draupadi** ([[Mahabharata]]): Both women are wronged by the system but respond differently — Antigone with defiance, Draupadi with questions that expose the system's contradictions.
> - **Antigone vs. Tara** ([[TARA play|Tara]]): Both are disabled by patriarchal structures — Antigone is legally punished for following her conscience, Tara is physically mutilated for being female.
> - **Creon vs. Faustus** ([[Doctor Faustus - Christopher Marlowe|Doctor Faustus]]): Both are proud men who overreach their authority, leading to catastrophic losses.

> [!NOTE] Theme: Duty vs. Authority
> The core tension in Antigone — divine law vs. civic law — mirrors:
> - The Dharma debate in [[Mahabharata]] (Rajdharma vs. personal duty)
> - [[Doctor Faustus - Christopher Marlowe|Faustus's]] rebellion against church authority
> - Draupadi's challenge to the authority of the dice game in [[In what ways does the Dicing episode provide a critique of the heroic ideal in the Mahabharata]]
