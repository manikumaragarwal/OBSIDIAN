# Introduction

Doctor Faustus is thought to be inspired from the Bible's [[Act's of Apostles 8.9-24]], the story of **Simon Magus**.  ^c0f806

references to witchcraft and magic in the Bible have always caused people to look upon the practice of magic as inviting eternal damnation for the soul.

In various legends, he was named **Heinrich Faust**, **Johann Faustus**, or **Georg Faust**

When the Renaissance came to northern Europe, Faust was made into a symbol of free thought, anticlericalism, and opposition to church dogma

There have been two version of Doctor Faustus : 
1. ***Christopher Marlowe***'s - 5 act **Play**, written in 1592.
2. ***Wolfgang Von Goethe***'s - 2 part dramatic Poem, written in 1808 & 1832 respectively.

#### Marlowe's version -
In Marlowe's version, Faustus makes a pact with Mephistopheles, trading his soul for '*24 years of supernatural power and pleasure*'. 
The play follows his story, of grand philosophical discussions to trivial pranks, and ends with his **damnation** as devil drags him to hell, despite his desperate last-minute pleas for redemption.

It's written as a renaissance tragedy, deeply theological and moralistic. according to Marlowe's version, There is no hope for redemption, once the pact with devil is formed. 


#### Goethe's version - 
In Goethe's version, Faustus makes a different pact with Mephistopheles: '*He will trade his soul, if he finds a moment so perfect, he wishes it to last forever, and this moment should combine extreme opposite emotions like love and hate at the same time*'. 
He knew that his true nature is of upward striving, and if devil helps him strive upward enough, he'd be one with god.

The first part of the poem, focuses on his tragic love affairs with an innocent Gretchen, whom he seduces and ultimately destroys. 
The second part of the poem talks about his journey through politics, art, classical mythology, and ultimately redemption. 

The tone of this version is very different from Marlowe's version, here Faustus is saved at the end, because his constant striving for knowledge (despite his mistakes), represent the best human quality. 

> [!NOTE] Aristotelian Tragedy Framework
> Doctor Faustus is a textbook [[Aristotle's Poetics|Aristotelian tragedy]]. See [[3 Tragic Beats]]:
> - **Peripeteia**: Faustus's damnation — his ambition and pride lead to ultimate reversal
> - **Anagnorisis**: The final monologue — Faustus realises he wasted his supernatural powers on trivial pranks
> - **Catharsis**: The audience feels both pity (for wasted potential) and terror (divine justice)

> [!TIP] Faustus as Tragic Hero — Compare across Texts
> - Like [[Mahabharata|Yudhishthira]], Faustus succumbs to a temptation that leads to catastrophic loss
> - Like [[Mahabharata|Karna]], Faustus is a figure of great potential brought low by a fatal choice
> - Unlike [[Antigone - Sophocles|Antigone]], Faustus does NOT have moral clarity — he knows he is wrong
> - Compare with the heroic ideal critique in [[In what ways does the Dicing episode provide a critique of the heroic ideal in the Mahabharata]]

> [!NOTE] The Two Faustuses — A Study in Redemption 
> Marlowe's Faustus = **no redemption** (Calvinist theology — predestined damnation) 
> Goethe's Faustus = **redeemed** (Romantic ideology — endless striving = virtue) 
> This connects to themes of [[PRIDE AND PREJUDICE|Pride and Prejudice]]: can a person truly change? Darcy's transformation echoes Goethe's Faustus.
