## MODEL ANSWER

**William Wordsworth’s sonnet _“Composed Upon Westminster Bridge, September 3, 1802”_ presents a rare and awe-inspiring vision of London at dawn. Although Wordsworth is known for celebrating nature, this poem is remarkable because he finds the same sublimity and emotional intensity in an urban landscape. The early morning scene transforms London into a space of peace, purity, and spiritual depth.**

Wordsworth begins with the emphatic declaration that “Earth has not anything to show more fair,” instantly establishing the extraordinary quality of the sight before him. The statement not only conveys the poet’s personal admiration but also reflects the Romantic emphasis on individual emotional experience. He insists that anyone who fails to appreciate this beauty must be “dull of soul,” reinforcing the Romantic belief that true perception requires emotional sensitivity.

A key feature of the poem is the imagery of **calmness and stillness**. Wordsworth describes the city as “silent, bare,” presenting London without noise, smoke, or movement. This is a striking contrast to the typical nineteenth-century industrial city. The listing of “ships, towers, domes, theatres and temples” gives a panoramic view of the city’s architecture. Placing sacred and secular buildings side by side symbolises a momentary harmony in urban life. Everything lies “open unto the fields and to the sky,” suggesting transparency, clarity, and unity with nature.

Nature plays a transformative role. The city “wears the beauty of the morning” like a “garment,” a metaphor that highlights the temporary purity given by natural light. The “smokeless air” enhances the sense of freshness—an unusual condition for London during the Industrial Age. Wordsworth even compares the sun steeping London in light to the way it illuminates “valley, rock, or hill,” suggesting that the urban landscape can equal natural scenery in beauty.

Personification intensifies the spiritual tone of the poem. The river “glideth at his own sweet will,” implying a freedom and grace typically associated with living beings. Even the houses “seem asleep,” turning the city into a peacefully resting organism. The final line, “And all that mighty heart is lying still,” is a powerful metaphor that presents London as a living heart—strong, vibrant, and essential—momentarily paused in tranquil silence.

The sonnet structure supports the theme. The octet describes the scene, and the sestet deepens the emotional impact. The steady rhythm mirrors the calmness of the city, while the steady flow of phrases mimics the gliding river.

**In conclusion, Wordsworth’s poem elevates an ordinary city into a moment of sublime revelation. Through imagery, personification, emotional depth, and Romantic ideals, the poem transforms London into a landscape of spiritual beauty. The sonnet stands as a reminder that the sublime can appear in unexpected places, even in the heart of a bustling metropolis, if one pauses long enough to see it.**
