## 🔑 PART A: How to Tackle This Question (Before Writing)

### 1️⃣ Understand the Key Phrase

The phrase **“Nathavati Anathavat”** literally means:

> _“Having lords, yet being without a protector.”_

This is **not just about Draupadi’s husbands**, but about:

- Failure of **patriarchal protection**
    
- Collapse of **dharma**
    
- Moral paralysis of powerful men
    

---

### 2️⃣ What the Examiner Expects

They want you to:

- Show **why** Draupadi is called “five-husbanded yet helpless”
    
- Analyse **each ‘protector’ figure’s failure**
    
- Connect her humiliation to **larger ethical breakdown**
    
- Show Draupadi as a **moral question**, not a victim stereotype
    

---

### 3️⃣ Structure to Follow (Very Important)

Your answer should move like this:

1. **Introduction**: Meaning of the phrase + relevance
    
2. **Draupadi’s position** as wife of five heroes
    
3. **Failure of each protector** (husbands, elders, king)
    
4. **Dice hall episode** as the core proof
    
5. **Larger thematic meaning**: dharma, womanhood, power
    
6. **Conclusion**: Why Draupadi exposes the hollowness of “protection”
    

This structure alone can fetch you **full marks** if written cleanly.

## ✍️ PART B: Model Answer (Write Like This in the Exam)

### **Draupadi as “Nathavati Anathavat”: A Study of Betrayed Protection**

Draupadi, one of the most powerful female figures in the Mahabharata, is often described as _“Nathavati Anathavat”_—a woman who has many lords yet remains without a true protector. Married to the five Pandava brothers, celebrated warriors and upholders of righteousness, Draupadi’s life presents a deep irony: **despite being surrounded by powerful men, she is repeatedly left unprotected at her most vulnerable moments**. This phrase thus becomes a sharp critique of patriarchal and moral failure rather than a comment on Draupadi’s marital status alone.

Draupadi’s position as the wife of five heroic men—Yudhishthira, Bhima, Arjuna, Nakula, and Sahadeva—should, in theory, guarantee her absolute security and honour. Each of the Pandavas represents strength, virtue, and royal authority. Yet the narrative reveals that **mere possession of power does not ensure moral courage**. Draupadi’s tragedy lies not in the absence of protectors, but in the **absence of timely action and ethical responsibility** from those meant to protect her.

This failure becomes most evident during the infamous dice-hall episode. Yudhishthira, bound by his obsession with gambling and a flawed understanding of royal dharma, wagers and loses Draupadi as property. His silence at this moment marks the first collapse of protection. Though he is her husband and king, he prioritises royal codes and personal weakness over his duty to his wife. Thus, the very man entrusted with upholding dharma becomes the agent of her humiliation.

The other Pandavas, though physically capable, remain silent spectators. Arjuna, the great warrior; Bhima, the embodiment of strength; and the twins, paragons of loyalty—all submit to the authority of dharma as interpreted by Yudhishthira, even when that interpretation is morally questionable. Their inaction demonstrates that **obedience without conscience can become complicity in injustice**. Draupadi’s multiple husbands, therefore, symbolise not multiplied protection but multiplied failure.

Beyond the Pandavas, the elders of the court—Bhishma, Drona, and Vidura—also fail Draupadi. Bhishma’s inability to decisively condemn her humiliation, citing the complexity of dharma, reveals how **ethical ambiguity can become an excuse for moral paralysis**. The court, filled with learned men and rulers, watches silently as Draupadi is dragged and insulted. In this moment, the entire social and political order stands exposed.

Draupadi’s powerful questioning in the assembly—asking whether Yudhishthira had the right to stake her after losing himself—marks her not as a helpless victim but as a **moral voice challenging patriarchal law**. Ironically, it is she, the woman without protection, who upholds the true spirit of dharma when all others fail.

Thus, the description of Draupadi as _“Nathavati Anathavat”_ becomes a profound commentary on the condition of women within patriarchal structures. It exposes the illusion that male guardianship guarantees safety and honour. Draupadi’s suffering reveals that **true protection lies not in power or numbers, but in moral courage and ethical action**.

### **Conclusion**

In conclusion, Draupadi is “with lords, yet without a protector” because her story reveals the tragic gap between **authority and responsibility**. The Mahabharata uses her humiliation to question social, political, and ethical systems, making Draupadi not merely a wronged woman, but a symbol of moral conscience in a world that repeatedly fails her.
