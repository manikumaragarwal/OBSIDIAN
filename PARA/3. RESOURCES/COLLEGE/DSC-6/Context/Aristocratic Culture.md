# Aristocratic Culture

The English aristocracy of the eighteenth century inhabited a world of leisure, politeness, and elaborate social ritual — a world simultaneously admired and satirised in the literature of the period.

## Key Features

- **Patronage system** — aristocrats supported poets, painters, and composers; writers navigated this economy carefully
- **Politeness and wit** — social life revolved around coffee-houses, assembly rooms, card parties, and salons; verbal dexterity was social currency
- **The marriage market** — aristocratic families managed alliances through carefully arranged marriages; female "honour" was inseparable from family interest
- **Leisure culture** — Hampton Court, Vauxhall Gardens, the races; conspicuous consumption marked rank
- **Duelling and honour** — aristocratic men settled disputes through ritual combat; reputation was defended publicly
- **Imported luxury** — foreign goods (Chinese lacquerware, Indian silks, Arabian spices) signalled wealth and empire

## Decline and Critique

By the mid-century, the merchant middle class was challenging aristocratic dominance economically. Satirists attacked:

- The gap between aristocratic *pretension* and moral reality
- The exploitation of social inferiors (servants, tenants, rural clergy)
- The frivolity of those who wielded real power

## In the Texts

- **[[The Rape of the Lock]]** — the poem's entire world is aristocratic London; Hampton Court, the card game, and Belinda's toilette all belong to this milieu; Pope both celebrates and gently satirises it
- **[[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]]** — Lilliput's court rituals, rope-dancing, and political appointments mirror English aristocratic corruption → [[Political Satire]]
- **[[The Vicar of Wakefield]]** — Squire Thornhill embodies the predatory aristocrat who exploits the rural poor with legal and sexual power
