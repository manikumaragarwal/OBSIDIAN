# Capital and Trade

The eighteenth century was Britain's age of commercial revolution. Expanding overseas trade, financial instruments, and mercantile empire transformed society and left a deep imprint on the literature of the period.

## Key Developments

- **South Sea Bubble (1720)** — speculative mania followed by financial collapse; ruined thousands of middle-class investors; backdrop to Pope's and Swift's writing
- **East India Company** — state-chartered monopoly that became an imperial power; brought Asian luxury goods into English domestic life
- **Transatlantic slave trade** — economic foundation of colonial profit; largely invisible in polite literature but implicit in its wealth
- **Rise of financial instruments** — stocks, bonds, annuities, and credit transformed who could accumulate wealth and who could lose it
- **Mercantile middle class** — tradesmen, merchants, and professionals whose status derived from commerce, not land or title

## Tension With Aristocratic Values

Land-owning gentry and the newer money of trade sat uneasily together. Satirists questioned whether commercial prosperity produced moral improvement or moral decay — the "luxury debate."

## In the Texts

- **[[The Rape of the Lock]]** — Belinda's dressing table is laden with exotic trade goods: "India's glowing gems," "Arabia's breath," "tortoise here and elephant unite" — empire enters domestic femininity through commerce; → [[Colonial Critique]]
- **[[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]]** — Swift anatomises colonial commerce as organised violence; Gulliver's speech to his Houyhnhnm master on European conquest names economic motive explicitly
- **[[The Vicar of Wakefield]]** — Dr Primrose's financial ruin stems directly from commercial vulnerability (a fraudulent merchant); economic precarity of the rural clergy is central to the plot
