# Jonathan Swift

Jonathan Swift (1667–1745) was an Anglo-Irish satirist, essayist, and clergyman — widely regarded as the greatest prose [[Satire|satirist]] in the English language. His work is animated by savage disillusionment with political institutions, imperial violence, and the pretensions of human reason.

---

## Biography

- Born in Dublin to English parents seven months after his father's death; raised in poverty
- Educated at Kilkenny School then **Trinity College Dublin** — awarded his B.A. only by "special grace," suggesting a rebellious student
- Employed as secretary to Sir William Temple at Moor Park, Surrey — encountered Whig intellectual culture and began writing
- Ordained as an Anglican clergyman; served in small Irish parishes; aspired to English preferment but was denied
- **Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin** (from 1713) — he regarded this as exile, but used the position to become Ireland's greatest polemical champion
- Active as a **Tory pamphleteer** during the Harley–Bolingbroke ministry (1710–14) — wrote *The Examiner* and key political tracts
- Suffered from **Ménière's disease** (labyrinthine vertigo and deafness) from middle age; declared of *unsound mind* in his last years
- Left his estate to found a hospital for the mentally ill in Dublin — St Patrick's Hospital (still operating)

### The Anglo-Irish Context

Swift's position is irreducibly bifurcated:
- English in culture, education, and aspiration
- Irish by birth, circumstance, and forced allegiance
- **The *Drapier's Letters* (1724–25)** — campaign against Wood's Halfpence (a copper coin scheme harmful to Irish economy) made him the idol of Dublin crowds
- **A Modest Proposal (1729)** — proposes eating Irish infants to solve poverty, making England's exploitation literal. The most sustained piece of ironic satirical writing in the English language. → [[PARA/3. RESOURCES/COLLEGE/DSC-6/Themes/Empire and Literature]], [[Colonial Critique]]

## Intellectual Context

- Profoundly sceptical of the [[Enlightenment Thought|Enlightenment's faith in human reason]] — *Gulliver's Travels* is in part a demolition of rational optimism
- Influenced by classical satirists: **Juvenal** (savage moral satire), **Horace** (courtly wit), **Lucian** (satirical dialogue and fantasy)
- Member of the **Scriblerus Club** with [[Alexander Pope]], Gay, Arbuthnot — mocked false learning and pedantry
- Read and responded to Descartes and the Royal Society; Part III of *Gulliver's Travels* directly targets the society's experimental projects
- Close friend of [[Alexander Pope]] until Pope's death

## The Misanthropy Debate

Swift's outlook has generated the central critical controversy about his work:

**The Misanthropic Reading**: Swift really does despise humanity; Gulliver's final rejection of his wife as a Yahoo and his preference for horses is Swift's own position.

**The Satirical Reading**: Gulliver is himself satirised — Stage 4 Gulliver (buying a horse and avoiding his family) is a portrait of reason gone to seed, not an endorsement of it. Swift wrote: *"I have ever hated all nations, professions, and communities, and all my love is towards individuals."*

**Key Evidence Against Misanthropy**:
- Swift's personal loyalties (Pope, Stella, Vanessa) were intense
- His Irish advocacy was active and consequential
- He said he wrote *Gulliver's* not "to vex the world, but to divert it"

For exam purposes: best to hold **both readings in tension** — Swift uses Gulliver's extremism to expose a real critique of human institutions. → [[Unreliable Narrator]], [[Human Nature in Swift]]

## Literary Style

- **Deadpan persona narration**: Swift speaks through masks (Gulliver, the *Proposer*, the *Drapier*)
- **Savage [[Irony]]**: controlled rage beneath apparent reasonableness — the *Proposal* says unspeakable things in a perfectly reasonable tone
- **[[Allegory]]** as political vehicle: Lilliput = England, Blefuscu = France, Big-Endians = Catholics
- Scale and proportion as satirical device: tiny people reveal human smallness; giant people expose human grossness
- Swift's prose is **clear, precise, and modern-sounding** — a deliberate rejection of ornate Baroque style

> "When a true genius appears in the world, you may know him by this sign: that the dunces are all in confederacy against him."
