# Alexander Pope

Alexander Pope (1688–1744) was the foremost English poet of the early eighteenth century, master of the [[Heroic Couplet]], and a defining voice of the [[Age of Reason]]. His life was shaped by profound personal and political marginalisation, which sharpened the satirical edge of his verse.

---

## Biography

- Born in London to a Roman Catholic linen draper; Catholicism defined his outsider status throughout his career
- As a Catholic, excluded from Oxford and Cambridge, from public office, from living within ten miles of London (Recusancy laws)
- Self-educated to mastery: taught himself Greek and Latin from scratch; read voraciously in French and Italian as well
- A childhood illness (likely Pott's disease of the spine) left him permanently hunched and never exceeding five feet tall — subjected to chronic vicious personal attacks
- Translated Homer's *Iliad* (1720) and *Odyssey* (1726) — earned him financial independence: the first English poet to live solely by his pen
- Settled at Twickenham (his villa and famous grotto) — a statement of literary independence from the London patronage world

### The Pope–Addison Quarrel

A crucial episode in 18th-century literary politics. Pope admired [[Joseph Addison]] early; Addison edited Ambrose Philips's pastorals and damned Pope's with faint praise. Pope suspected Addison of working to undermine *The Rape of the Lock*. The relationship soured into open literary war. Pope's devastating portrait of Addison as **"Atticus"** in *An Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot* (1735) is one of the period's great satirical set-pieces:

> "Damn with faint praise, assent with civil leer, / And without sneering, teach the rest to sneer."

The quarrel illustrates the fraught relationship between literary talent and cultural gatekeepers in the early 18th century — and Pope's refusal to accept a subordinate position.

## Intellectual Context

- Shaped by [[Enlightenment Thought]] — order, decorum, wit, the authority of reason
- Classical models: Homer, Virgil, Horace, Dryden — whom he regarded as the master of the couplet
- Engaged with the competitive [[Rise of the Public Sphere|literary marketplace]] (Grub Street, booksellers, subscriptions)
- Member of the **Scriblerus Club** (c. 1714) alongside [[Jonathan Swift]], Gay, Arbuthnot — a collaborative satirical project mocking pedantry and false learning; produced *Memoirs of Martinus Scriblerus*
- Catholic faith placed him in tension with Whig culture and the Hanoverian settlement

## Craft and Form — The Heroic Couplet

Pope's mastery of the [[Heroic Couplet]] is the central fact of his literary legacy. Key features:

- **Antithesis**: contrasting ideas balanced within or across a couplet — e.g. *"True wit is nature to advantage dressed, / What oft was thought, but ne'er so well expressed."*
- **[[Zeugma]]**: yoking two incongruous objects under one verb — e.g. *"Or stain her honour, or her new brocade."*
- **Bathos**: deliberate descent from the elevated to the trivial
- **Proportion**: the couplet form itself embodies Augustan values of balance and self-control
- **Sound**: Pope's ear for internal rhyme, alliteration, and onomatopoeia is unmatched in the period

His famous dictum: *"True ease in writing comes from art, not chance, / As those move easiest who have learned to dance."*

## Catholic Identity and Marginalisation

Pope's Catholicism is inseparable from his literary position:
- **Outsider perspective** — provided the critical distance essential for satire
- **Twickenham villa** — carved out a private world of literary authority precisely because the public world was closed to him
- **The Dunciad** — a partly sectarian war: the dunces Pope attacks are often Whig, Protestant, Grub Street hacks
- Connects to theme of [[PARA/3. RESOURCES/COLLEGE/DSC-6/Themes/Empire and Literature]] — Pope writes from the margin of power, but shapes taste at the centre
