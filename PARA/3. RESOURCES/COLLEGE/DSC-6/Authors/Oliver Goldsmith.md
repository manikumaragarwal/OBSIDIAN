# Oliver Goldsmith

Oliver Goldsmith (c. 1728–1774) was an Anglo-Irish novelist, poet, playwright, and essayist — a figure of extraordinary versatility who produced some of the most-loved works of the 18th century while living in near-permanent debt and social precariousness.

---

## Biography

- Born in County Roscommon (or Longford), Ireland; father was an Anglo-Irish curate — modest, rural background
- Educated (erratically) at **Trinity College Dublin**; admitted as a sizar (poor scholar) — reportedly unhappy and undistinguished
- Attempted and failed: medicine (Edinburgh, Leiden), law, clerical orders
- Wandered through Europe (France, Italy, Switzerland) with almost no money; reportedly played his flute for lodging
- Arrived in London c. 1756 — initially worked as an apothecary's assistant, then a school usher
- Entered the literary marketplace through hack work for Ralph Griffiths and then [[Samuel Johnson]]'s circle

### Poverty and Debt

Goldsmith's poverty was lifelong and notorious:
- Despite considerable earnings from *The Vicar of Wakefield*, *The Deserted Village*, and *She Stoops to Conquer*, he was almost always in debt
- Johnson famously sold the manuscript of *The Vicar of Wakefield* to a bookseller to rescue Goldsmith from arrest for rent arrears
- He died in debt, aged ~45 — Johnson's epitaph: *Nullum quod tetigit non ornavit* ("He touched nothing that he did not adorn")
- His personal penury gives authenticity to his sympathy with dispossessed rural poor in *The Deserted Village*

### Periodical Journalism

Before the major works, Goldsmith was a prolific journalist:
- **Chinese Letters** (1760–61), later *The Citizen of the World* — mock-Chinese observer satirising English society; influenced by Montesquieu's *Persian Letters* and Voltaire
- Contributions to *The Bee*, *The Critical Review*, and many booksellers' compendiums
- The journalistic persona of the "outsider" (Chinese traveller, wandering Irishman) shaped his ironic distance in the novel

## Intellectual Context

- Wrote during the **transition from Augustan to pre-Romantic sensibility** — combines rational clarity with emotional sympathy
- Influenced by both [[Enlightenment Thought]] and the rising [[Sentimental Novel]] tradition (Richardson, Sterne)
- The **Augustan inheritance**: clear prose, [[Irony]], satire of pretension
- The **sentimental strand**: benevolence, feeling, the virtuous poor, moral sentiment (Shaftesbury, Hutcheson)
- Deeply concerned with **enclosure and displacement** — *The Deserted Village* attacks the rural depopulation caused by [[Capital and Trade|commercial enclosure]]

## Goldsmith's Irony: The Central Debate

The critical debate about Goldsmith hinges on whether *The Vicar of Wakefield* is:

**A Sentimental Novel**: Dr Primrose is a genuinely virtuous man whose faith and patience are rewarded; the novel affirms Christian providence and the moral order.

**An Ironic Novel**: Primrose is a self-deluded, vain clergyman; his moral essays about women are ignored by his family; his suffering is partly self-inflicted through naïveté; the providential ending is too neat to be believed.

**Goldsmith's Balance**: He appears to hold both simultaneously — the novel is *warm but not naive*, sentimental but not credulous. The irony is gentle, not savage (unlike Swift). This balance is the characteristic Goldsmith mode.

Key evidence for irony:
- Primrose publishes essays on monogamy while his wife wears diamonds and his daughters dress as ladies
- His "stoic patience" is undercut by his constant new disasters
- The Gothic villain Thornhill is resolved too conveniently

## The Deserted Village as Social Critique

*The Deserted Village* (1770) is essential context for the novel:
- Attacks **commercial enclosure**: landlords converting agricultural land to private pleasure grounds, displacing rural communities
- "Sweet Auburn" (the lost village) — idealised as a site of community, simplicity, and moral virtue
- The poem argues that **luxury and trade corrode rural England**: the displaced poor emigrate to "the torrid or the frozen zone"
- Connects to [[Capital and Trade]], [[Social Hierarchy in 18th Century England]]: a pre-Romantic anti-commercial critique

---

## Literary Style

- **Gentle [[Irony]]**: never savage, always warm; the reader is invited to smile at Primrose, not despise him
- **Sympathetic character portraits**: Olivia ruined but still loved, Moses/George naïve but good-hearted
- Clear, elegant **prose style** — readable and direct, unlike Sterne's digressive complexity
- **[[Sentimental Novel|Sentimentalism]]** expressed through tears, forgiveness, family reunion — but tempered by comic self-awareness
