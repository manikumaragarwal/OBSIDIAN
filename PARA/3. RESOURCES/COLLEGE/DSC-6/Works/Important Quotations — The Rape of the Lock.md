# Important Quotations — The Rape of the Lock

Key quotations from [[The Rape of the Lock]] by [[Alexander Pope]], organised by canto and theme.

---

## Canto I — The Toilette

> "What dire offence from am'rous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things"
> — I.1–2
Opens the poem with [[Mock Epic|mock-epic]] grandeur; signals the ironic distance between "mighty contests" and a snipped lock. [[Irony]], [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Triviality vs. Grandeur.

> "Say what strange motive, Goddess! could compel / A well-bred Lord t'assault a gentle Belle?"
> — I.7–8
The mock-epic invocation sets the tone for the trivial conflict. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Social decorum, Gender roles.

> "Sol through white Curtains shot a tim'rous Ray, / And op'd those Eyes that must eclipse the Day"
> — I.13–14
Hyperbolic description of Belinda's awakening, emphasizing her beauty and vanity. [[Hyperbole]], Theme: Beauty, Vanity.

> "If to her share some Female Errors fall, / Look on her Face, and you'll forget 'em all."
> — I.17–18
[[PARA/3. RESOURCES/COLLEGE/DSC-6/Themes/Gender Politics]]: beauty excuses all; female worth reduced to appearance. [[Satire]], Theme: Beauty, Superficiality.

> "For when Coquettes to twenty now arrive, / They grow good-humour'd, and begin to live."
> — I.19–20
Satirical comment on the fleeting nature of youthful coquettery and the societal expectations placed on women. [[Satire]], Theme: Age, Female roles.

> "This Nymph, to the destruction of mankind, / Nourish'd two Locks, which graceful hung behind"
> — I.27–28
Hyperbolic description of Belinda's hair, foreshadowing its significance. [[Hyperbole]], Theme: Beauty, Power of women.

> "Th' inferior Priestess, at her Altar's side, / Trembling, begins the sacred Rites of Pride."
> — I.127–128
Belinda's maid, Betty, is depicted as a priestess assisting in the "sacred Rites of Pride" (her toilette). [[Mock Epic]], [[Satire]], Theme: Vanity, Rituals of beauty.

> "A heav'nly Image in the Glass appears, / To that she bends, to that her Eyes she rears."
> — I.125–126
Belinda's self-admiration, highlighting her narcissism. [[Narcissism]], Theme: Self-obsession, Beauty.

> "Unnumber'd Treasures ope at once, and here / The various Off'rings of the World appear;"
> — I.129–130
Description of the exotic goods used in Belinda's toilette, satirizing materialism and global trade for vanity. [[Hyperbole]], Theme: Materialism, Global trade.

## Canto II — The Sylphs

> "Not with more Glories, in th' Ethereal Plain, / The Sun first rises, than in Belinda's Train"
> — II.1–2
Belinda's beauty is compared to the rising sun, elevating her to a divine status in a mock-heroic manner. [[Mock Epic]], [[Hyperbole]], Theme: Belinda's grandeur, Vanity.

> "Fair Nymphs, and well-drest Youths, around her shone, / But ev'ry Eye was fix'd on her alone."
> — II.5–6
Emphasizes Belinda's captivating beauty and the attention she commands. Theme: Beauty, Attention.

> "Love in these Labyrinths his Slaves detains, / And mighty Hearts are held in slender Chains."
> — II.21–22
Metaphor for the power of beauty and flirtation to ensnare admirers. [[Metaphor]], Theme: Love, Power of beauty.

> "This Day, black Omens threat the brightest Fair, / That e'er deserv'd a watchful Spirit's Care;"
> — II.101–102
Ariel's prophecy of impending doom for Belinda, adding a sense of mock-epic suspense. [[Foreshadowing]], Theme: Fate, Danger.

> "Whether the Nymph shall break Diana's Law, / Or some frail China Jar receive a Flaw;"
> — II.105–106
[[Zeugma]]: equates moral honour (chastity) with a fragile material object, exposing the conflation of values in [[Aristocratic Culture]]. [[Zeugma]], Theme: Chastity vs. Materialism.

> "Or stain her Honour, or her new Brocade"
> — II.107
Another classic [[Zeugma]]: equates moral honour with fabric, further highlighting the superficiality of aristocratic values. [[Zeugma]], Theme: Honour, Materialism.

> "Or lose her Heart, or Necklace, at a Ball"
> — II.109
Another zeugma: the heart and a necklace treated as equivalent losses, underscoring the trivialization of emotions. [[Zeugma]], Theme: Love, Materialism.

> "For this, ere Phoebus mounts the purple Skies, / Belinda still must shun the Walks of Men."
> — II.111–112
Ariel's warning to Belinda, implying that her beauty makes her vulnerable and requires constant protection. Theme: Protection, Vulnerability.

> "The Sylphs behold it with a fearful Gaze, / And tremble for the Fate of ancient Lace."
> — II.117–118
The sylphs' concern for material possessions rather than Belinda's moral well-being. [[Satire]], Theme: Materialism, Superficiality.

## Canto III — The Rape

> "At ev'ry Word a Reputation dies."
> — III.16
Satirical comment on the destructive power of gossip and slander in polite society. [[Satire]], Theme: Gossip, Reputation.

> "The hungry Judges soon the Sentence sign, / And Wretches hang that Jury-men may Dine;"
> — III.21–22
A biting social commentary on the corruption and indifference of the justice system. [[Satire]], Theme: Justice, Corruption.

> "Belinda now, whom Thirst of Fame invites, / Burns to encounter two advent'rous Knights,"
> — III.25–26
Belinda's desire for social triumph, framed in mock-heroic terms as a battle. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Fame, Competition.

> "The Knave of Diamonds tries his wily Arts, / And wins (oh shameful Chance!) the Queen of Hearts."
> — III.87–88
Description of the card game (Ombre) using martial and romantic language, elevating the trivial game to epic proportions. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Games, Strategy.

> "The Peer now spreads the glitt'ring Forfex wide, / T'inclose the Lock; now joins it, to divide."
> — III.147–148
The scissors rendered in [[Mock Epic|epic martial imagery]]; the trivial act dignified and thereby ridiculous. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Violence, Violation.

> "Then flash'd the living Lightning from her Eyes, / And Screams of Horror rend th' affrighted Skies."
> — III.155–156
Belinda's exaggerated reaction to the cutting of her lock, using epic imagery for a trivial event. [[Hyperbole]], [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Exaggeration, Loss.

> "Not louder Shrieks to pitying Heav'n are cast, / When Husbands, or when Lap-dogs breathe their last;"
> — III.157–158
A satirical comparison of the loss of a husband or a lap-dog to the loss of a lock of hair, highlighting the triviality of aristocratic concerns. [[Satire]], [[Zeugma]], Theme: Triviality, Grief.

## Canto IV — The Cave of Spleen

> "Here in a Grotto, shagg'd with horrid Thorn, / Trembling she stood, and never durst return."
> — IV.15–16
Description of the Cave of Spleen, a place of melancholy and ill-humour, reflecting Belinda's emotional state. [[Allegory]], Theme: Melancholy, Emotions.

> "Men, States, and Empires, fall then in a Jilt."
> — IV.10
A cynical view of the power of women and their caprices to influence grand affairs. [[Satire]], Theme: Power of women, Social influence.

> "For when the Fair long absent have been miss'd, / And in their Places, Spades, or Cards, or Dice, / Or any thing, that's not a Woman, rise."
> — IV.47–50
A satirical jab at the superficiality of social interactions, where women are interchangeable with games. [[Satire]], Theme: Social life, Superficiality.

> "As when a Hero in the Trojan Field, / With mighty Arms, his God-like Foe repell'd,"
> — IV.69–70
A mock-epic simile comparing Belinda's rage to a hero in battle. [[Mock Epic]], [[Simile]], Theme: Anger, Battle.

> "Oh hadst thou, Cruel! been content to seize / Hairs less in sight, or any Hairs but these!"
> — IV.173–174
Belinda's lament, revealing her vanity and the specific importance of the stolen locks. Theme: Vanity, Loss.

> "Then flash'd the living Lightning from her Eyes, / And Screams of Horror rend th' affrighted Skies."
> — IV.155–156 (Note: This is a repeat from Canto III, but often cited in Canto IV context for Belinda's rage)
Belinda's dramatic reaction to the loss of her lock, emphasizing her exaggerated emotions. [[Hyperbole]], [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Exaggeration, Rage.

## Canto V — The Apotheosis

> "To arms, to arms! the fierce Virago cries, / And swift as Lightning to the Combat flies."
> — V.37–38
Belinda's call to battle, transforming her into a "Virago" (a fierce woman) in a mock-heroic fight. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Battle, Female agency.

> "While thro' the Press enrag'd Thalestris flies, / And scatters Deaths around with both her Eyes,"
> — V.43–44
Thalestris, Belinda's friend, joins the fray, depicted as a formidable warrior. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Battle, Female support.

> "When thro' the world fair Belinda's name shall fly, / And all mankind shall praise her matchless eye."
> — V.11–12
Ariel's prophecy of Belinda's lasting fame, ironically tied to her beauty. Theme: Fame, Beauty.

> "Not fierce Othello in so loud a Strain / Roar'd for the Handkerchief that caus'd his Pain."
> — V.105–106
A satirical comparison of Belinda's rage to Othello's, trivializing tragic passion. [[Allusion]], [[Satire]], Theme: Rage, Triviality.

> "This Lock, the Muse shall consecrate to Fame, / And 'midst the Stars inscribe Belinda's Name!"
> — V.149–150
The lock ascends to heaven as a constellation — [[Irony|ironically]] granting immortality to the trivial. [[Mock Epic]], [[Irony]], Theme: Immortality, Triviality.

> "For ever, and for ever, shalt thou shine, / A Star among the lesser Lights of Heav'n;"
> — V.143–144
The lock's apotheosis, ensuring Belinda's lasting fame through this trivial incident. [[Mock Epic]], Theme: Immortality, Fame.
