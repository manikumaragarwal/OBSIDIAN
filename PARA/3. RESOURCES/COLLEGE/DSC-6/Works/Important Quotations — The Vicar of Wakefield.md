# Important Quotations — The Vicar of Wakefield

Key quotations from [[The Vicar of Wakefield]] by [[Oliver Goldsmith]], with comprehensive analysis.

---

## On Family and Virtue

> "I was ever of opinion, that the honest man who married and brought up a large family, did more service than he who continued single, and only talked of population."

[[Dr Primrose]]'s opening statement establishes his core domestic and moral philosophy. This sentiment, while seemingly virtuous, immediately introduces a touch of [[Irony]] and [[Comic Naivete]] that will define his character. His pride in this opinion, and his subsequent large family, sets up the central conflict of the novel as his ideals are tested by misfortune and the corrupting influence of the outside world. It highlights the 18th-century emphasis on [[Family Morality]] and social contribution through procreation, but also foreshadows the challenges such a large, vulnerable family will face.

---

## On Suffering and Resilience

> "We have lived as we ought, and have learned to be content with little."

This quote, often repeated by the vicar, encapsulates his [[Stoicism]] and unwavering faith in the face of escalating adversity. It reflects a core tenet of [[Christian Morality]] and [[Patience]] — the belief that virtue will be rewarded, if not in this life, then in the next. However, Goldsmith imbues this acceptance with a subtle, [[Comic Naivete]] as the vicar continually finds new misfortunes to endure, often with a slightly self-congratulatory air. It's a testament to his spiritual resilience but also a source of gentle humor regarding his perpetual optimism.

> "The only way to conquer fortune is to endure it."

A more active declaration of the vicar's philosophy of endurance. This isn't passive acceptance but a strategic approach to life's trials. It speaks to a profound belief in the power of [[Patience]] and [[Resilience]] as virtues, suggesting that by steadfastly bearing one's burdens, one ultimately triumphs over them, if only spiritually. This stoic outlook is central to the novel's exploration of [[Suffering and Redemption]].

---

## On Social Inequality and Deception

> "The same ambition that makes a great man often makes a villain."

This profound observation reflects on the moral dangers inherent in unchecked power and social aspiration, particularly within the [[Aristocratic Culture]] of 18th-century England. It directly anticipates the character of [[Squire Thornhill]], whose ambition and social standing enable his villainy. The quote critiques the superficiality of status and wealth, suggesting that true greatness lies not in power but in virtue, a theme central to Goldsmith's social commentary on [[Social Hierarchy in 18th Century England]].

> "Our greatest security lies in our being unknown."

Spoken by the vicar after their move to the new parish, this highlights the family's vulnerability and their attempt to escape past misfortunes and the scrutiny of a judgmental society. It underscores the precariousness of their social position and the desire for anonymity as a form of protection against the machinations of the powerful. This sentiment is ironic, as their very obscurity makes them targets for deception.

---

## On Appearance vs. Reality

> "The world in general is not half so bad as it is imagined."

This optimistic declaration by the vicar, often made despite overwhelming evidence to the contrary, showcases his [[Comic Naivete]] and unwavering faith in human goodness. It's a recurring motif that highlights the gap between his idealized view of the world and the harsh realities his family faces. While admirable, this perspective often leaves him vulnerable to the deceptions of characters like [[Squire Thornhill]] and [[Mr. Burchell]], who initially appears as a humble wanderer.

> "A man's character is not to be judged by his clothes."

A classic statement on the deceptive nature of outward appearances, particularly relevant in a society obsessed with social distinctions. This quote champions the idea that true worth lies in inner virtue rather than external display. It's a lesson the Primrose family learns repeatedly, often to their detriment, as they are swayed by the superficial charm of the wealthy and dismiss the genuine kindness of the humbly dressed. This theme is central to the novel's critique of [[Social Hierarchy in 18th Century England]].

---

## On Forgiveness and Redemption

> "I always loved my daughter, and now I love her more than ever."

The vicar's unconditional forgiveness of [[Olivia Primrose]] after her seduction and apparent ruin is a pivotal moment, embodying the [[Sentimental Novel]] ideal of boundless parental love and Christian charity. This act of grace stands in stark contrast to the harsh societal judgments of the time, emphasizing compassion over condemnation. It highlights the theme of [[Redemption]] and the power of familial bonds to overcome shame and despair.

> "To be good is to be happy; to be virtuous is to be wise."

This aphorism, frequently uttered by the vicar, encapsulates his core moral philosophy. It links virtue directly to happiness and wisdom, suggesting that true contentment comes from living a righteous life, regardless of external circumstances. It serves as a guiding principle for the Primrose family and reinforces the novel's didactic message about the ultimate triumph of [[Christian Morality]] and inner goodness over worldly trials.
