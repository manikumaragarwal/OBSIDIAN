# 18th Century English Literature

The eighteenth century was the age that trusted reason more than feeling. Shaped by the [[Enlightenment Thought|Enlightenment]] and broadly called the [[Age of Reason]], the period produced a literature defined by wit, formal precision, and a deep suspicion of human folly. Where earlier writers might have turned to revelation or romance, the Augustans turned to the page as a mirror — and what they saw, they laughed at, or wept over, or anatomised in a [[Heroic Couplet|couplet]] of savage elegance.

[[Satire]] became the century's dominant voice, practised in very different registers. [[Jonathan Swift]] used it like a scalpel — cold, clinical, unsparing. His [[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]] exposes the absurdity of political institutions through the logic of a travel narrative, while [[A Modest Proposal - Johnathan Swift|A Modest Proposal]] turns the reasoned language of economic pamphlets against itself, forcing the reader to see what polite society refuses to see. [[Alexander Pope]], working in verse rather than prose, brought the same critical impulse to the drawing room. [[The Rape of the Lock]] applies the full weight of [[Mock Epic|mock-epic]] machinery to a quarrel over a lock of hair — the gap between grandeur and triviality *is* the satire. [[Oliver Goldsmith]] is gentler than either; his [[Irony]] is wry rather than furious, and [[The Vicar of Wakefield]] reads almost as a warm portrait of human self-deception rather than a condemnation of it.

What ties these writers together is less a single tone than a shared set of concerns. The [[Rise of the Public Sphere]] — coffeehouses, literary journals, a growing reading public — gave writers a genuinely new kind of audience, and they knew it. Literature was no longer just for courts; it addressed citizens. This shift created conditions in which the [[Political Satire|political pamphlet]], the novel, and the verse essay could all become serious forms of public argument. At the same time, the expansion of the [[British Empire in the 18th Century|British Empire]] and the culture of [[Capital and Trade|trade and commerce]] reshaped social life in ways that writers couldn't ignore — [[Social Hierarchy in 18th Century England|social hierarchy]] was both more rigid and more anxious than it looked on the surface.

The century also marked a quiet revolution in prose fiction. The [[Sentimental Novel]] — with its emphasis on feeling, domestic virtue, and [[Family Morality|family]] — emerged partly as a counterweight to Augustan rationalism. Goldsmith belongs here, but so in many ways does Jane Austen, whose [[PRIDE AND PREJUDICE|Pride and Prejudice]] (just over the century's edge) inherited the tradition's irony while deepening its psychological range. ## Major Authors

- [[Alexander Pope]] — Horatian wit, supreme command of the [[Heroic Couplet]], master of the [[Mock Epic]]
- [[Jonathan Swift]] — Juvenalian fury, deadpan persona, radical scepticism about [[Human Nature in Swift|human nature]]
- [[Oliver Goldsmith]] — gentle [[Irony]], sentimental warmth, moral observation

## Major Works

- [[The Rape of the Lock]] — society satire through mock-heroic verse
- [[Gulliver's Travels - Jonathan Swift]] — political allegory as travel narrative
- [[The Vicar of Wakefield]] — [[Sentimental Novel|sentimental fiction]] and moral comedy
- [[A Modest Proposal - Johnathan Swift|A Modest Proposal]] — the [[Political Satire|political pamphlet]] taken to its savage extreme

## Historical & Intellectual Context

- [[Age of Reason]] — the philosophical framework for Augustan literature
- [[Enlightenment Thought]] — reason, order, progress as governing values
- [[Enlightenment Rationality]] — and its limits, as Swift in particular explored
- [[Rise of the Public Sphere]] — the new literary marketplace and reading public
- [[British Empire in the 18th Century]] — colonial expansion as background and target
- [[Bourgeois Society]] — the rising middle class reshaping culture
- [[Capital and Trade]] — commerce transforming social relations and rural life
