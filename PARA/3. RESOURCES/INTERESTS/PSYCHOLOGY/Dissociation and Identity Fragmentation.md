## What Dissociation Actually Is (In Human Terms)

Imagine for a moment you're watching a scary movie. When it gets too intense, don't you sort of... check out? Your mind goes somewhere else?

That's a mild form of dissociation. Everyone does it.

But when trauma happens - especially when you're young, like you were at 14 - your mind doesn't just "check out" for a moment. It creates a full strategy. It says:

**"This is too much to handle. I'm going to disconnect me from it."**

And so your mind pulls apart from:
- Your body (depersonalization)
- Your emotions ("I feel nothing")
- Your memories ("I can't remember")
- Your sense of self ("I don't know who I am")

It's not "going crazy." It's your brain's way of protecting you from experiences too big for one human to process all at once.

## Something Important About Your Trauma Memory

You mentioned you've tried to forget what happened at 14, thinking forgetting is the best possible way.

Here's what research shows us: **You can't actually forget trauma.** You can dissociate from it, compartmentalize it, put it away - but it doesn't disappear.

Instead, what happens is:
- It leaks out in your behavior (compulsive lying, dissociation, anxiety)
- It leaks out in your relationships (trust issues, fear of intimacy)
- It leaks out in your self-perception (shame, feeling defective, identity confusion)

The alternative to "forgetting" isn't permanent suffering. It's **integrating**.

Processing trauma means:
- You acknowledge what happened
- You feel the emotions (not all at once, gradually)
- You understand it wasn't your fault
- You integrate it into your story in a way that doesn't destroy you

The goal isn't to erase what happened. The goal is to live fully again despite what happened.
