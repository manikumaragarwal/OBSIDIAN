> *When you tell the audience what to think, they observe; when you let them infer it, they experience.*

## What It Is
"Show, don't tell" is the principle that an audience should arrive at conclusions through observation of action, specific detail, and subtext, rather than being handed the information via direct statement. The act of inference bridges the gap between the screen (or page) and the audience, making them an active participant in the story rather than a passive recipient.

## Why It Works (The Psychology)
This relies on a psychological phenomenon similar to the Ikea Effect: we place disproportionately higher value on things we assemble ourselves. When an audience member has to connect the dots to realize a character is heartbroken, they *own* that realization. It bypasses intellectual processing and hits emotionally. When you simply state "he was heartbroken," you collapse the experience into raw data, pausing the narrative momentum.

## How It Works in Practice
- **Concrete Detail over Abstraction:** Instead of saying a character is messy, describe "three pizza boxes stacked beside a monitor." (The detail also reveals specific character traits that "messy" lacks).
- **Action over Description:** Instead of stating a character is brave, show them stepping in front of a threat.
- **Subtext over Statement:** Dialogue should rarely state exactly what a character is feeling. People mask their feelings; dialogue should reflect that mask, forcing the audience to read the subtext.
- **The Specificity Principle:** "A mug" is telling. "A NASA mug with a chip in the handle, filled to the brim even at 2pm" is showing. Specificity creates texture and implies history.
- **When Telling is Right:** The rule is not absolute. Telling is crucial for fast-paced plot transitions, comedic timing, or crystallizing the final thematic lesson of a story. Do not waste time showing mundane details that don't matter.

## Movie Examples
- **Parasite:** The film never has a character explicitly state "the Parks are incredibly wealthy and oblivious." It shows it through the absurd size of their lawn, the careless way they throw around money, and the physical architecture of the house.
- **Whiplash:** Andrew's descent into obsession is never explained via a monologue. It is shown through bleeding hands, cracked cymbals, ice baths, and ignored phone calls. We infer his madness from his behavior.

## YouTube / Content Examples
- **B-Roll:** Good B-roll doesn't just cover cuts; it shows the emotional reality of what the voiceover is discussing without needing to explicitly state it.
- **Case Studies:** Instead of saying "this company was failing," a creator shows the empty parking lots and the desperate discount signs.

## Common Beginner Mistakes
- Telling emotions instead of showing the physical behaviors those emotions cause (e.g., "She felt sad" vs. "She stared at the wall for an hour").
- "As you know, Bob" exposition, where characters tell each other things they both already know just for the audience's benefit.
- Explaining your own metaphors or jokes immediately after delivering them, robbing the audience of the inference.

## Exercises to Practice
- Take a descriptive paragraph that relies entirely on telling (e.g., "He was a very angry man who hated his job."). Rewrite it to convey the exact same information using only physical action and specific props.
- Write a scene where two characters are furious at each other, but they are in a public space and cannot raise their voices or address the fight directly.
