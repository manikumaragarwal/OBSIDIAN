# Parasite — Storytelling Analysis

> *Capitalism doesn't just create poverty — it makes the poor invisible to each other.*

## What It Is
A poor Korean family infiltrates the lives of a wealthy family with catastrophic consequences. Directed by Bong Joon-ho, the film uses genre-bending—from dark comedy to thriller to horror to tragedy—as a storytelling tool to escalate stakes, mystery, and emotion simultaneously.

## Why It Works (The Psychology)
The film leverages Kishōtenketsu structure, where the basement revelation acts as the 'ten' (twist/development) that recontextualizes everything. It reveals the structural juxtaposition that there was always a class below the poor. The conflict isn't just rich vs. poor, but poor vs. poor fighting for proximity to wealth.

## How It Works in Practice
- **The Hourglass:** The scholar's rock (geumsuseok), the smell, the underground family — all planted and buried in the richness of the social commentary, hiding their true significance.
- **The Crystal Ball:** "The Parks are nice because they're rich. If I had money, I'd be nice too." This plants the question of whether niceness and wealth are inseparable. The film answers brutally.
- **Dramatic Irony:** When the Kim family discovers the Moons in the basement, the audience suddenly knows far more than the Parks. We watch a family unknowingly living above a desperate man.

## Movie Examples
- **The Smell Scenes:** Devastating emotional beats where the Parks discuss the Kim family's smell as if they're furniture. The Kim family overhears, and the audience feels the violence of the contempt.
- **The Basement Revelation:** Not just a plot twist, but a structural juxtaposition (Kishōtenketsu) showing the invisible underclass.
- **Kim Ki-taek's Arc:** The father begins servile and aspirational. He ends by going underground — literally inhabiting the space he was always invisible in. The contrast between beginning and end is the core theme.
