# Scene Design
> *A scene is a unit of change. If nothing changes, the scene shouldn't exist.*

## What It Is
A scene is a discrete unit of story that moves something forward—plot, character arc, tone, relationship dynamics, or information. The absolute best scenes manage to move several of these elements simultaneously. A scene is not just a location or a conversation; it is a structural building block designed to escalate the story.

## Why It Works (The Psychology)
Audiences process stories in discrete chunks of action and reaction. A well-designed scene manages cognitive load by having a clear entry point, a focal point of conflict, and an exit that leaves an emotional residue. When scenes fail to change anything, the audience's brain registers a lack of progress, resulting in boredom.

## How It Works in Practice
Before writing any scene, a storyteller must answer **The Five Questions**:
1. **What changes in this scene?** (If the answer is nothing, cut the scene immediately).
2. **Who wants what?** (The core character desire driving the scene).
3. **What's at stake if they don't get it?** (The scene-level stakes).
4. **What emotional state should the audience be in at the scene's END?** (The designed emotional beat).
5. **What is not being said?** (The subtext carrying the real weight).

### Core Principles of Scene Design
- **The Escalation Principle:** Every scene *must* increase stakes, mystery, or emotion. It cannot just maintain the status quo.
- **Entry and Exit:** Enter the scene as LATE as possible (in medias res) to avoid boring setup. Exit the scene as EARLY as possible. Do not linger once the change has occurred.
- **Scene Transitions:** How one scene's emotional state sets up the next. The emotional residue of a scene provides the context for whatever follows.
- **Visual vs. Written Storytelling:** In film, a director can show two things simultaneously. In writing, you must sequence them. The sequencing *is* the meaning.
- **Dialogue:** The subtext is always below the surface. What characters deliberately avoid saying generates the scene's kinetic energy.
- **The Pressure Test:** If you removed this scene, would the story still make sense and feel complete? If yes, strongly consider cutting it. If no, keep it.

## Common Beginner Mistakes
- **Information Transfer Scenes:** Scenes that exist purely to dump exposition or transfer information between characters, rather than to create an experience for the audience.
- **Zero Pressure:** Scenes where characters just hang out with no internal conflict, no immediate desire, and no stakes.

## Exercises to Practice
- Take a single scene from a film or book you love. Identify the exact entry point, the exact exit point, what fundamental shift occurs, and what specific emotion it leaves the audience with at the end.
