# Chekhov's Gun demands that every planted detail fires
> *If you show a loaded gun in the first act, it must fire by the third; every significant element introduced must serve a narrative purpose.*

## What It Is
Chekhov's Gun is the principle that every significant element introduced must serve a purpose. If you show a gun in act 1, it must fire by act 3. It's a rule of narrative economy. The inverse is also true: if it fires in act 3, it must have been shown in act 1.

## Why It Works (The Psychology)
It works based on narrative economy and payoff satisfaction. We naturally assign meaning to what stories show us. If a story shows it, we believe it matters. Every detail that doesn't pay off creates confusion or disappointment. Every detail that DOES pay off creates the feeling of inevitability. Chekhov's Gun honors audience expectation.

## How It Works in Practice
- **Loaded vs. Unloaded Guns:** Not every detail needs to be a gun. Some details are atmosphere. The skill is knowing which is which—and being honest with your audience.
- **Subverting with the Hourglass:** The hourglass technique subverts Chekhov by burying the gun in a room full of objects that look equally significant, exploiting the rule. The gun was always there, we just couldn't find it.
- **The Emotional Chekhov's Gun:** An emotional state introduced early must resolve—either by changing or by tragically failing to change. 

## Movie Examples
- **Parasite:** The scholar's rock (introduced as a gift, becomes a literal weapon).
- **No Country for Old Men:** The cattle bolt (introduced in a slaughterhouse context, becomes the signature murder weapon).
- **The Prestige:** The diary (introduced as a mystery, becomes the key to the ultimate reveal).
- **Oldboy:** The hypnosis reveal (every interaction with Mi-do is a loaded gun that fires devastatingly at the end).
- **Whiplash:** Andrew's terror of mediocrity is an emotional Chekhov's Gun that Fletcher fires over and over.

## YouTube / Content Examples
- **Engineering / Build Videos:** Showing a random piece of hardware at the start that ends up saving the entire project at the end.
- **Stand-up Comedy:** The callback. Setting up a premise early that becomes the punchline at the end.

## Common Beginner Mistakes
- **Chekhov's Arms Room:** Introducing too many guns, leading to massive confusion.
- **Forgetting a planted gun:** Setting up a significant detail and abandoning it.
- **Firing an unloaded gun:** Having a crucial object suddenly appear in the climax to save the day without establishing it earlier (Deus ex machina).

## Exercises to Practice
- Watch a film you haven't seen before. Write down every significant object introduced in act 1. After the film, check which ones fired.
